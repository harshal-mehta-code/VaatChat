Words have a gender Masculine, feminine, or neuter — you can often hear it in the ending.

AudioGujaratiRomanEnglish / contextVerdict
છોકરો chhokro boy
masculine
છોકરી chhokri girl
feminine
છોકરું chhokru child
neuter
— no audio — — RULE — Every Gujarati noun has a gender. You can often hear it in the ending: ‑ો is usually masculine, ‑ી feminine, and ‑ું neuter. Gender isn't only about male vs. female — objects have a gender too — but it quietly decides how the words around a noun change.
— no audio — — CONTRAST — English gave up grammatical gender centuries ago. Gujarati kept all three — including a neuter that many Indian languages lost. Here, it's something you'll actually hear.

"My" changes shape The word for "my" dresses to match what you own.

AudioGujaratiRomanEnglish / contextVerdict
મારો ભાઈ maaro bhai my brother
brother — masculine
મારી બહેન maari bahen my sister
sister — feminine
મારું નામ maaru naam my name
name — neuter
— no audio — — RULE — "My" isn't a single word in Gujarati — it changes to match the gender of what you own: મારો before a masculine word, મારી before a feminine word, મારું before a neuter word. It's the same ‑ો / ‑ી / ‑ું pattern you just met.
— no audio — — CONTRAST — In English, "my" never changes — my brother, my sister, my name. In Gujarati it dresses to match the noun. Get the ending right and you instantly sound like you belong.

The verb goes last Gujarati saves the action for the end of the sentence.

AudioGujaratiRomanEnglish / contextVerdict
હું પાણી પીઉં છું hu paani piu chhu I drink water (lit. I water drink)
મને ચા ભાવે છે mane chaa bhaave chhe I like tea (lit. to-me tea is-pleasing)
તે રોટલી ખાય છે te rotli khaay chhe She eats roti (lit. she roti eats)
— no audio — — RULE — Gujarati saves the verb for the end. Where English says "I drink water," Gujarati says "I water drink" — હું પાણી પીઉં છું. The doer comes first, then the thing, and the action lands last.
— no audio — — CONTRAST — English order: I — drink — water. Gujarati order: I — water — drink. Once your ear expects the verb last, whole sentences fall into place.

Two ways to say "you" તું for warmth, તમે for respect — it matters with family.

AudioGujaratiRomanEnglish / contextVerdict
તું કેમ છે? tu kem chhe? How are you? (to a close friend or child)
casual, warm
તમે કેમ છો? tame kem cho? How are you? (to an elder)
respectful
— no audio — — RULE — Gujarati has two words for "you." તું is warm and casual — for close friends, siblings, and children. તમે is respectful — for elders, in-laws, and people you don't know well. The verb even shifts with it: છે becomes છો.
— no audio — — CONTRAST — English lost this — everyone is just "you." In a Gujarati family it matters: તમે to Ba is basic good manners, and getting it right is how you win the in-laws over.

Actions in the present A verb plus a છું/છે/છો helper that matches who's doing it.

AudioGujaratiRomanEnglish / contextVerdict

તે ખાય છે te khaay chhe He/she eats

— no audio — — RULE — The present tense is a verb form plus a "to be" helper — છું for હું (I), છે for તે (he/she), છો for તમે (you, respectful). It's the very same છું/છે/છો you met in કેમ છો?
— no audio — — CONTRAST — English barely marks the person (I eat, he eats). Gujarati changes the verb and adds a matching helper — but you already know the helper from "how are you?"

Saying "not" One tidy word — નથી — covers am not, is not, are not.

"In" comes after ઘર + માં = ઘરમાં — the "in" attaches to the end of the noun.

AudioGujaratiRomanEnglish / contextVerdict

બજારમાં bajaarmaa in the market
— no audio — — RULE — Where English puts "in" before the noun, Gujarati attaches માં after it: ઘર + માં = ઘરમાં. These little words come after, so they're called postpositions.
— no audio — — CONTRAST — English: in the house. Gujarati: house-in (ઘરમાં). The idea is the same; it just hangs off the back of the word.

The past's famous twist In the past, the verb agrees with the thing done — not the doer.

AudioGujaratiRomanEnglish / contextVerdict
મેં રોટલી ખાધી me rotli khaadhi I ate roti
રોટલી feminine → ‑ી
મેં ભાત ખાધો me bhaat khaadho I ate rice
ભાત masculine → ‑ો
મેં પાણી પીધું me paani peedhu I drank water
પાણી neuter → ‑ું
— no audio — — RULE — In the past of a "doing-to" verb, the verb agrees with the thing done to, not the doer — and the doer takes a special form (હું → મેં). So મેં રોટલી ખાધી (‑ી, feminine roti) but મેં ભાત ખાધો (‑ો, masculine rice). The food drives the ending.
— no audio — — CONTRAST — English "I ate" never changes, whatever you ate. Gujarati flips the logic in the past: the verb bends to the object. Nail this and you've cracked the sentence learners find hardest — the real "I understand Gujarati" moment.
